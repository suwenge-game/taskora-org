Division integrity: the integer constraint as a weapon

Division is the rarest operator in the answer bank and the most powerful constraint on the board. Learning its divisibility triggers turns ÷ greens into instant structural wins.

In this guide · Divisibility triggers, compressed · Two-step division chains · Division as elimination · Mini mode corner cases

Divisibility triggers, compressed

The classic triggers cover nearly every board case: even last digit for 2; last two digits divisible by 4 for 4; digit-sum divisible by 3 and 9; ending in 0 or 5 for 5; alternating digit-sum for 11. On an eight-slot board these are not school trivia — they are instant candidate filters.

When a ÷ green appears, write the divisor beneath the board and apply the trigger to every candidate in one pass. Candidates failing the trigger are eliminated without arithmetic; the survivors are already halfway verified.

Two-step division chains

Longer equations sometimes chain division: a÷b×c or a×b÷c. Order matters because intermediate integrality is required — a÷b must be whole even if the final product is. When reading candidates, evaluate left to right and mark the first non-integer intermediate as dead; do not let a clean final result distract from a broken middle.

Conversely, when constructing probe candidates, choose chains where the division lands on a clean multiple of the multiplication result. The bank generator does the same, which is why such candidates are over-represented among real answers.

Division as elimination

Because ÷ is rare in the bank, a board with no ÷ feedback for three rows makes ÷-containing candidates progressively unlikely. This is elimination by absence — the quietest information on the board. When the pool refuses to narrow and no ÷ has appeared, reorder your candidate list to test non-division structures first.

The reverse holds: a single ÷ green certifies the answer belongs to the small integer-clean family, and the pool collapses around divisibility pairs. Either way, division status is worth checking explicitly on every row, not just when it is green.

Mini mode corner cases

Mini's six slots compress operands to single digits, so ÷ behaves differently: the result is always a single digit, which means the dividend is at most 9×9. Memorize the81-entry grid lazily — you already know most of it from times tables — and mini division boards become pure pattern recognition rather than arithmetic.

The grid also serves classic mode: any two-digit dividend appearing in a candidate should factor into single-digit multiples you already know, or the candidate is suspect.

Practice sequencing for this material

Skills from this page land in this order: first run the drill on a worksheet set (timed, untidy is fine), then apply it to the daily equation the same day, then teach it back — a written sentence in your notes explaining the rule to a hypothetical beginner. The teaching-back step is the retention mechanism; skips there decay within a week.

Expect a three-day consolidation window per skill. Day one feels mechanical, day two feels ambiguous, day three it starts firing unprompted. Players who quit on day two never reach the payoff, which is why this site spaces its worksheet sets rather than batching them — spacing is the method, not a compromise.

Fair-difficulty guarantee

The daily equation is generated under constraints this site documents openly: integer division results, standard precedence, and a deterministic daily seed. No guesswork spikes, no hidden difficulty days. If a board feels unfair, check two things before suspecting the puzzle: every division in your candidate must be integer-clean at each step, and every mixed-operator reading must follow standard precedence. Those two checks resolve the overwhelming majority of "impossible" boards.

The same constraints make the site's advice durable: because the generator's rules are fixed and published, techniques derived from them cannot be patched out from under you. What changes is the calendar, not the mathematics.
