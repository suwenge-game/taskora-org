Candidate enumeration: from feedback to a finite list

The solving skill that scales is enumeration: converting a row of feedback into an explicit candidate list you can hold on one page. Here is the disciplined procedure.

In this guide · Write the constraints before the candidates · Template-first enumeration · Ranking the survivors · When to stop enumerating

Write the constraints before the candidates

Feedback gives four constraint families: positional greens, present-elsewhere yellows with forbidden slots, absent letters, and structural facts (equals position, operator identities). Write all four families in shorthand before naming a single candidate — the writing prevents the classic error of satisfying three constraints while violating the fourth.

The shorthand becomes fluent within a week: greens as letters with slot numbers, yellows as letter-excluded-slot pairs, structure as a template string like DD×D=D D. One line each. The whole board state fits on two lines of text.

Template-first enumeration

Enumerate structural templates first — the shapes the left side can take given known greens — then fill digits. Template-first enumeration never misses a candidate family, while digit-first enumeration wanders. With two operator templates live, each digit-filling pass is a bounded loop: pick the leftmost open slot, try its candidates, recurse.

The discipline feels slow for two rows and then becomes the fastest way to play, because enumeration quality is exactly what the endgame consumes. Boards stop feeling lucky or unlucky; they feel like paperwork.

Ranking the survivors

Once the candidate list is explicit, rank by the information each guess would buy if wrong — the coverage instinct from the frequency guide, applied to a finite list. Candidates that differ from the leader in exactly one high-frequency letter are strong second guesses; candidates that duplicate the leader's tested letters are dead weight.

Ranking also exposes the gamble case honestly: if the list has five entries and no guess separates them, the arithmetic from the endgame guide applies. Enumeration turns gambling into calculation.

When to stop enumerating

Enumeration has a stop condition: when the remaining candidates all share the same tested letters and differ only in arrangement, further enumeration adds nothing — switch to the probe design from the endgame guide. Conversely, when the list still exceeds a page, enumerate structural templates again instead of digits; the split is in the structure, not the digits.

The stop conditions are what make enumeration sustainable. Enumerate until the list is useful, then play. The board, not the procedure, decides when that moment arrives.

Practice sequencing for this material

Skills from this page land in this order: first run the drill on a worksheet set (timed, untidy is fine), then apply it to the daily equation the same day, then teach it back — a written sentence in your notes explaining the rule to a hypothetical beginner. The teaching-back step is the retention mechanism; skips there decay within a week.

Expect a three-day consolidation window per skill. Day one feels mechanical, day two feels ambiguous, day three it starts firing unprompted. Players who quit on day two never reach the payoff, which is why this site spaces its worksheet sets rather than batching them — spacing is the method, not a compromise.

Fair-difficulty guarantee

The daily equation is generated under constraints this site documents openly: integer division results, standard precedence, and a deterministic daily seed. No guesswork spikes, no hidden difficulty days. If a board feels unfair, check two things before suspecting the puzzle: every division in your candidate must be integer-clean at each step, and every mixed-operator reading must follow standard precedence. Those two checks resolve the overwhelming majority of "impossible" boards.

The same constraints make the site's advice durable: because the generator's rules are fixed and published, techniques derived from them cannot be patched out from under you. What changes is the calendar, not the mathematics.
