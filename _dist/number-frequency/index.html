Digit and operator frequency guide

Frequency is a tie-breaker, not an answer key. Use general equation patterns to build informative legal guesses, then let tile evidence take control.

In this guide · Rules · Strategies · Daily · Basics

Why exact percentages are misleading here

A digit’s apparent frequency depends on the candidate-building rules, equation length, permitted operands, result range, operator mix, and whether equivalent expressions are retained. Changing any of those choices changes the counts. This guide therefore avoids invented percentages and focuses on stable relationships that follow from legal whole-number equations.

Frequency should never outrank direct feedback. A green character is fixed, a gold one is required elsewhere, and gray feedback limits unused copies. Those facts are specific to the current answer; a general pattern is not.

Patterns among digits 0–9

All ten digits can appear in legal operands or results, but they do not play identical roles. Zero is useful inside or at the end of a multi-digit number, yet cannot begin one. It also cannot be a divisor. One participates in many additions and subtractions and is a factor of every whole number, while multiplication or division by one can leave magnitude unchanged.

Even digits have more opportunities to participate in exact division by 2. Digits that form multiples of 5 often end in 0 or 5, which creates recognizable result patterns. Larger digits can create wider products, while smaller operands make it easier to keep multiplication inside a short character limit. These are arithmetic constraints, not promises that a particular digit appears today.

Operator availability

OperatorGeneral behavior in a valid pool
AdditionFlexible across many operand pairs; may widen the result by a carry.
SubtractionMust keep the final result non-negative and often creates a shorter result.
MultiplicationDepends on factor pairs and can expand result width quickly.
DivisionMost constrained because the divisor cannot be zero and the step must be exact.

This does not justify assuming addition is always the hidden operator. A good first equation can test two operator types when the eight-character structure supports them. In later rows, operator colors and possible positions matter far more than pool-wide tendencies.

Common equation shapes

Character limits favor compact relationships. A two-digit operand combined with a one-digit operand can leave room for an operator, equals sign, and short result. Two two-digit addends can fit when the result and sign position cooperate, as in 12+34=46. A one-digit result often points toward subtraction or exact division, but other structures may also produce it.

Mixed-operator expressions offer broad testing but consume a position that could otherwise hold a digit. Multiplication can turn small operands into a two-digit result. Exact division reflects factor pairs: once a divisor is known, plausible dividends are multiples of it. Learn these shapes so you can enumerate candidates after the equals sign or result width becomes fixed.

Turn frequency into an opening

  1. Choose a legal equation shape that fits the selected mode.
  2. Prefer distinct digits where the arithmetic permits, because each tests a new character.
  3. Include useful operator variety without creating a false equation.
  4. Avoid unnecessary repeated digits on the first row.
  5. Calculate with precedence and recount every character before submitting.

After the first row, stop optimizing generic coverage. Carry forward greens, relocate golds, remove exhausted gray copies, and respect duplicate quantities. If two remaining candidates satisfy all feedback equally, then arithmetic patterns can help choose the candidate that tests a new operator, digit, or result width.

A practical narrowing example

Suppose the equals sign is green and the answer ends in a green 6. You now know the expression length and part of its target. A gold plus sign must be moved to another operator position, while gray digits with no matched duplicate should be removed. Build a short list of true equations ending in 6 that fit those placements. Only after applying those hard constraints should you favor a candidate with broader new-digit coverage.

Practice path

First, write five true addition and subtraction equations of the active length using mostly distinct digits. Next, replace one with multiplication and one with exact division. Then use practice prompts to explore several left sides for the same result. Finish with Mini mode, where fewer positions make equation shapes easier to recognize, before returning to Classic Daily play.

Frequency can suggest what to test. Only arithmetic validity and color feedback tell you what the current answer can be.

Frequently asked questions

Is there one best starting digit?

No. Broad legal coverage is usually more useful than betting on one digit.

Is division as flexible as addition?

No. It is constrained by factor relationships and exact whole-number results.

Should frequency override tile colors?

Never. Current-board evidence is stronger than general tendencies.
